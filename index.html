Welcome. If you're watching this, chances are you know your subject inside out — you've worked in the field, you've solved real problems, and you've been asked to bring that expertise into the classroom. What you might not have had much exposure to is the paperwork that sits behind every module you teach: the module descriptor.

By the end of this session, you'll be able to:

  • Read a module descriptor and actually understand what it's asking of you
  • Explain what learning outcomes are and how they shape what you teach and how you assess
  • Understand how NFQ levels set the standard for your module
  • Work out roughly how much effort your students should be putting in, based on credit value
  • Spot the practical teaching and assessment implications hiding inside a descriptor

Think of the module descriptor as the contract between you, your students, and the institution. Once you can read it fluently, a lot of the "why do I have to do it this way" questions in academic life start to make sense.

Section 1 · 5 min

What is a module descriptor, and why it matters

A module descriptor is not paperwork for its own sake — it's the blueprint for everything that happens in your classroom.

Let's start with a plain definition. A module descriptor is a formal, approved document that describes a single unit of study — a module — within a programme. It typically includes:

Title, code & credits
The module title, its code, and its credit value
NFQ level
The National Framework of Qualifications level
Learning outcomes
What students should know, understand, or be able to do by the end
Indicative content
The syllabus — the topics to be covered
Teaching methods
The teaching and learning methods used
Assessment strategy
How students will demonstrate they've achieved the outcomes
Requisites & reading
Prerequisites, co-requisites, and reading lists

You might be handed this document on day one and think of it as a formality. In practice, it's the single most important document for planning your teaching, because it has already been through a quality assurance process — reviewed, validated, and approved by the institution and, in many cases, external panels.

Why it matters

🛡️

It's a quality assurance instrument. Every module goes through an approval process to meet institutional and national standards. QQI's Core Statutory Quality Assurance Guidelines and related Award Standards set the expectation that what's taught and assessed matches what was approved.

📋

It's your teaching brief. The descriptor tells you what students are entitled to learn and how they'll be assessed — regardless of who's teaching the module in any given semester. This protects consistency and fairness.

🤝

It's a two-way commitment. Students, quality reviewers, external examiners, and professional bodies all rely on the descriptor to know what a module promises. Drift too far from it, and you risk misaligning the module with what's being formally assessed.

🧭

A useful metaphor The module descriptor is the destination and the route map. How you drive — your teaching style, your industry stories, your delivery techniques — is entirely up to you. But the destination (the learning outcomes) and the general route (assessment methods, content areas) are fixed by the descriptor.

Section 2 · 7 min

Learning outcomes: what they are and how they drive everything

Learning outcomes are the engine of the module descriptor. Everything else — content, teaching methods, assessment — exists to serve them.

A learning outcome is a statement of what a student should know, understand, or be able to do by the end of a module — and be able to demonstrate it.

Notice the emphasis: not what you will teach, but what the student will be able to do. This is a subtle but important shift for anyone coming from an industry training background, where content delivery is often the main focus.

How learning outcomes are written

Well-written learning outcomes usually follow a simple structure:

For example:

  • "Evaluate the structural integrity of a proposed building design against current safety codes."
  • "Design a basic relational database to meet a specified business requirement."

The action verb matters enormously. It signals the level of cognitive demand expected — and this is where Bloom's Taxonomy comes in.

  • Remember — recall facts (e.g. "list," "define")
  • Understand — explain ideas (e.g. "describe," "summarise")
  • Apply — use knowledge in new situations (e.g. "demonstrate," "implement")
  • Analyse — break information into parts (e.g. "compare," "differentiate")
  • Evaluate — make judgements (e.g. "critique," "justify")
  • Create — produce something new (e.g. "design," "develop")

If a learning outcome uses the verb "evaluate," your teaching and your assessment both need to give students a genuine opportunity to practise evaluating — not just recall facts. A multiple-choice test alone wouldn't cut it.

How learning outcomes drive curriculum design

Once outcomes are set, everything else should flow from them:

  1. Content is selected because it's needed to achieve the outcome — not the other way around.
  2. Teaching methods are chosen because they give students the chance to practise the skill or thinking implied by the outcome.
  3. Assessment is designed to let students demonstrate the outcome, at the right level of challenge.
🔍

Your practical filter When deciding how to spend limited contact hours, ask: "Does this activity help students get closer to demonstrating this outcome?" If not, it may be interesting — but it might not be essential.

Section 3 · 7 min

NFQ levels: setting the standard

The NFQ level tells you how much independence, complexity, and critical thinking to expect — and to demand — from your students.

Every module descriptor states an NFQ level — a reference to Ireland's National Framework of Qualifications, maintained by QQI (Quality and Qualifications Ireland). The NFQ has 10 levels, from Level 1 (basic) through to Level 10 (doctoral).

The level isn't just a label; it comes with a detailed set of expectations called level indicators, published by QQI, covering:

  • Knowledge — breadth and kind
  • Know-how and skill — range and selectivity
  • Competence — context, role, learning to learn, insight

What changes as the level rises

Here's the practical version, in plain language. Select a level to see what it means and a realistic example task:

6789
Higher Certificate

Guided application of established methods

Students work with a defined body of knowledge and apply known procedures in familiar contexts, usually with structured guidance. The focus is on doing things correctly by following an established process.

Example taskFollow a defined process to troubleshoot a fault in a piece of equipment.
Ordinary Bachelor Degree

Applying knowledge to less predictable problems

Students take on broader knowledge and begin to select appropriate methods for problems that aren't entirely routine. They can work with some autonomy within known boundaries.

Example taskChoose and apply an appropriate method to solve a moderately complex, real-world problem.
Honours Bachelor Degree

Independent analysis and evaluation

Students are expected to work independently, analyse and evaluate information critically, and handle some ambiguity. They can bring together ideas and justify their reasoning.

Example taskCritically analyse competing solutions to a problem and justify a recommended approach.
Master's Degree

Original, self-directed problem-solving

Students operate at the edge of current knowledge, dealing with ambiguity and incomplete information. They can design original solutions and defend them with rigour.

Example taskDesign and justify an original solution to an ambiguous, open-ended industry problem.

Why this matters for your teaching

This is often the biggest adjustment for lecturers coming from industry. In the workplace, you're usually solving problems using deep expertise and giving clear, authoritative answers. In the classroom, your job is to design experiences that let students build toward that expertise — appropriate to their level.

If you're teaching a Level 6 module, expecting first-year students to critically evaluate competing theoretical frameworks independently may be pitched too high — they haven't yet built the foundational knowledge. Conversely, if you're teaching a Level 9 module and still spoon-feeding definitions, you're likely under-challenging students relative to what the award standard requires.

📚

Find your discipline's Awards Standards. QQI publishes Awards Standards for many disciplines, translating general NFQ level indicators into subject-specific expectations. They give concrete guidance on what graduates at a given level should know and do. Your programme's quality office or programme director can point you to the relevant document.

Section 4 · 6.5 min

ECTS credits and student workload

Credits are not a measure of how much you teach. They're a measure of how much time a typical student should spend learning.

Every module carries a credit value, usually expressed in ECTS — the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System. It's a European-wide system designed to make student workload comparable and transferable across institutions and countries.

⚖️

The key conversion to remember 1 ECTS credit ≈ 20–25 hours of total student effort. The exact figure is set by each institution (commonly 20–25 hours in Irish HE, based on the European Commission's ECTS Users' Guide).

That "total student effort" figure includes everything — not just time in your classroom:

🏫 Contact hours
Timetabled lectures, labs, workshops, tutorials
📖 Independent study
Reading and self-directed learning
✏️ Assessment work
Preparation for and completion of assessments
👥 Group work
Collaboration outside class
📝 Exam prep
Revision and the exam itself

Working the numbers

Let's do an example together. A module is worth 5 ECTS credits, at 20 hours per credit. Total student effort = 5 × 20 = 100 hours across the semester. If timetabled contact hours are 24 hours (2 hours/week × 12 weeks), that leaves 76 hours of independent study, assignment work, and exam preparation the student is expected to do on their own.

Why this matters for you as a teacher

🧾

Sanity-check your assignment load. If you set three 2,000-word essays plus weekly quizzes plus a group project on a 5-credit module, ask whether that realistically fits within the independent study hours available — alongside everything else the student is doing across their other modules that semester.

🎯

Set realistic expectations, not just easier ones. The workload figure isn't about making things easy — it's about matching demand to credit value, so students can plan their time sensibly and your module isn't inadvertently over- or under-weighted compared to others.

There's a well-established line of research on this — including work associated with the European Commission's ECTS Users' Guide, and broader higher-education workload research (including work often associated with Kember on student workload and time-on-task) — which consistently shows that students' perceived workload and actual time spent don't always match what course designers intend. Being deliberate about credit-to-hours mapping is one of the best tools you have to keep this realistic.

💡

Quick practical tip When designing an assignment, try timing how long it would reasonably take a competent student to complete it — reading, drafting, and finishing. It's rough, but it catches a lot of over- or under-loaded assessments before they become a problem.

Section 5 · 2 min

Bringing it together: reading a descriptor in practice

Let's put it all together. When you next open a module descriptor, read it with these four questions in mind. Expand each for a practical tip you can apply when planning a class.

Check the verbs against Bloom's Taxonomy to gauge the level of thinking required.

💡 Underline every action verb in the outcomes before you plan a single class — they tell you the cognitive level to aim for.

This is constructive alignment in practice.

💡 For each class activity, name the outcome it serves. If you can't, question whether it earns its place.

Match your expectations of independence to the level indicators.

💡 Ask: am I doing the thinking for them, or designing tasks that make them do it at the right level?

Do the credit-to-hours maths before finalising your assessment brief.

💡 Time yourself doing a scaled-down version of the task, then multiply — a quick reality check on load.

✅

If you can answer all four with confidence, you're not just complying with a quality assurance requirement — you're teaching in a way that gives your students the best chance of success, and makes the most of the deep expertise you bring from industry.

Section 6 · Summary & assessment

Summary, final knowledge check & reflection

The module descriptor is your map. Learning outcomes are your destination. NFQ level sets your altitude. ECTS credits tell you how far the journey should take.

Let's recap

Module descriptor
An approved document defining what a module covers, how it's taught, and how it's assessed — it exists to ensure quality and consistency for students.
Learning outcomes
Describe what students should be able to do, not just what you'll teach. Bloom's helps you read the level of thinking; Biggs' constructive alignment keeps outcomes, teaching, and assessment pointed the same way.
NFQ levels
Set the standard for independence, complexity, and critical thinking — and QQI's Awards Standards translate this into discipline-specific expectations.
ECTS credits
Represent total student effort, not just contact time — a practical check for setting realistic assessment workloads.
