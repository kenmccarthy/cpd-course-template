Understanding Module Descriptors

~1.5 min read

You've built a career on solving real problems, making judgement calls under pressure, and knowing your field at a level most people only read about. That expertise is exactly what your students need. What this session gives you is the other half of the picture: the mechanics of higher education that sit underneath every module you teach — starting with the document that defines it, the module descriptor.

This isn't abstract theory for its own sake. Everything here is pitched at the level you'll actually be operating at as a lecturer, in that it is critical, evaluative, and grounded in practice.

By the end of this 30-minute session, you'll be able to:

  • Interpret a module descriptor with confidence
  • Explain the role of learning outcomes and how they shape teaching and assessment
  • Understand how the National Framework of Qualifications (NFQ) levels influence expectations and standards
  • Estimate realistic learner effort based on credit values
  • Identify key teaching and assessment implications from a module descriptor

Throughout, you'll be asked to connect what you're learning to your own industry background — because your professional judgement is a genuine asset in curriculum thinking, and this session is designed to help you use it deliberately.

Pitched at NFQ Level 8. The language, concepts and tasks in this session are calibrated to honours bachelor degree standard — the level most part-time and industry-based lecturers teach at.

Section 2 of 7

What is a module descriptor, and why it matters

~4.5 min read

A module descriptor is not paperwork for its own sake. It is the approved blueprint for everything that happens in your classroom.

A module descriptor is a formal, quality-assured document describing a single unit of study within a programme. It typically sets out:

Title, code & credit value
What the module is called, its code, and what it's worth in credits
NFQ level
The National Framework of Qualifications level the module is pitched at
Intended learning outcomes
What students should be able to demonstrate by the end
Indicative content
The topics the module is expected to cover
Teaching & learning methods
How the module is delivered
Assessment strategy
How students demonstrate they've achieved the outcomes
Requisites & reading
Prerequisites, co-requisites, and reading lists

An analogy from your world Think of the module descriptor as comparable to a professional specification document or a signed-off scope of work. Just as you wouldn't deliver a project that quietly departs from an agreed spec, a module shouldn't drift far from its descriptor without going through a formal change process.

Why a module descriptor matters

It's a quality assurance instrument. Every module has passed through an approval process to ensure programmatic integrity, which means what's delivered matches what was approved.

It's your teaching brief. It defines what students are entitled to learn and how they'll be assessed, regardless of who is teaching in a given semester, thereby protecting consistency and fairness.

It's a shared reference point. Students, external examiners, professional accreditation bodies, and quality reviewers all rely on it as the single source of truth for the module.

Section 3 of 7

Learning outcomes: what they are and how they drive everything

~7 min read

Learning outcomes are the engine of the module descriptor. Content, teaching, and assessment all exist to serve them.

A learning outcome states what a student should know, understand, or be able to do by the end of a module, along with the ability to demonstrate it. The emphasis is on demonstrable capability, not just coverage of content.

How learning outcomes are written

At Level 8, look for higher-order verbs You'll typically see outcomes built around evaluate, critique, design, synthesise, justify — verbs that reflect the critical and independent thinking expected of honours degree graduates.

Reading the cognitive demand of a verb

Bloom's Taxonomy, as revised by Anderson and Krathwohl, is the standard reference for reading how much thinking a verb is asking for:

  • Remember / Understand — recall, explain
  • Apply — use knowledge in new situations
  • Analyse — break down, compare, differentiate
  • Evaluate — judge, critique, justify
  • Create — design, develop, synthesise

At Level 8, outcomes cluster heavily around the top three tiers — students are expected to do more than reproduce knowledge; they're expected to interrogate it.

Constructive alignment

John Biggs' constructive alignment is the principle that good teaching happens when three elements point in the same direction:

  • Learning outcomes — what students should achieve
  • Teaching and learning activities — what you actually do
  • Assessment tasks — how students prove achievement

If an outcome says "evaluate," but your assessment only tests recall, there's a misalignment — and students are set up to fail through a design flaw, not a lack of effort.

Bringing in your industry lens

You almost certainly evaluate, critique, and justify decisions constantly in your professional life — assessing a design, a proposal, a diagnosis, a piece of work against a standard. That's precisely the kind of thinking a Level 8 learning outcome is asking students to develop.

Make your reasoning visible One of the most valuable things you can do as an industry-based lecturer is to make that professional judgement process visible to students — showing them not just your conclusion, but how you got there.

Section 4 of 7

NFQ levels: setting the standard

~6.5 min read

The NFQ level tells you how much independence, complexity, and critical judgement to expect from your students.

Every module descriptor states an NFQ level, drawn from Ireland's National Framework of Qualifications, maintained by QQI. The NFQ runs from Level 1 to Level 10.

It also aligns with the European Qualifications Framework (EQF) — the broader European system that allows qualifications to be compared and recognised across countries. Irish NFQ Level 8 corresponds to EQF Level 6, and this alignment is what allows a graduate's qualification to be understood and trusted internationally.

[Confirm the level-by-level NFQ–EQF alignment against QQI's current published comparison chart before publishing.]

What Level 8 specifically expects

Since this session — and likely much of your teaching — sits at Level 8, it's worth dwelling here. QQI's level indicators for Level 8 describe graduates who can:

  • Apply a broad and detailed body of knowledge, some of it at the current boundaries of the field
  • Analyse and evaluate information critically, including handling incomplete or ambiguous information
  • Exercise independent judgement and take responsibility for decisions in unpredictable contexts
  • Communicate complex ideas effectively to specialist and non-specialist audiences

The sharpest adjustment for industry professionals

Your instinct, quite reasonably, is to give students the right answer based on your expertise. At Level 8, your role shifts: you're not just handing over the answer; you're designing situations where students practise the independent judgement that got you to that answer in the first place.

Find your discipline's Awards Standards. QQI's discipline-specific Awards Standards translate these general level indicators into concrete expectations for your field. It's worth locating the document relevant to your discipline — your programme coordinator or quality office can point you to it — as it gives a much more precise picture of what a Level 7, 8 or 9 graduate in your area should know and be able to do.

Section 5 of 7

ECTS credits, EQF and student workload

~7 min read

Credits are not a measure of how much you teach. They're a measure of how much time a typical student should spend learning — and they're what makes qualifications comparable across Europe.

Every module carries a credit value in ECTS — the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System.

ECTS sits alongside the EQF as one of the tools that makes higher education qualifications comparable and transferable across European countries. A Level 8 honours degree is typically 240 ECTS credits in total, giving both students and employers a consistent basis for understanding the scale and standard of a qualification, wherever it was earned.

The key conversion 1 ECTS credit ≈ 20–25 hours of total student effort. The precise figure is set institutionally, per the European Commission's ECTS Users' Guide.

Total student effort includes everything:

Timetabled contact hours
Lectures, labs, workshops, tutorials
Directed learning
Set tasks outside contact hours
Independent study
Self-directed study and reading
Assessment work
Preparation for and completion of assessments
Group work
Collaboration outside class
Exam preparation
Revision and the exam itself, if required

Working the numbers

A 5-credit module at 20 hours/credit = 100 hours total student effort. If contact hours are 24 (2 hrs/week × 12 weeks), then directed learning, independent study, assessment preparation and completion, group work outside class and exam preparation come to 76 hours.

Why this matters for you

Two practical checks:

Sanity-check assessment load. At Level 8, students may be managing several modules simultaneously, each with its own credit-hour budget. An assessment strategy that's reasonable in isolation can become unmanageable in aggregate.

Match demand to credit value, not to your own standards from industry. In professional practice, workload often expands to meet the task at hand. In a credit-bound curriculum, the task has to be scoped to fit the available hours — an important distinction.

Research associated with the European Commission's ECTS Users' Guide, and broader higher education workload literature (including work often associated with Kember (2004) on student workload and time-on-task), consistently shows a gap between designers' intended workload and students' actual time-on-task. Being deliberate about the credit-to-hours mapping is one of your best tools for closing that gap.

Reflection · industry link · not scored

Estimate, from your own experience, how long it takes a competent professional to complete a task similar to one you're considering setting as an assessment. Now compare that to the independent study hours available under the module's credit value. Where might the two be out of step — and in which direction?

Bringing it together: reading a descriptor in practice

~2 min read

When you next open a module descriptor, read it against four questions. Expand each for a practical tip you can apply when planning.

Check the verbs against Bloom's Taxonomy to gauge the level of thinking required.

Underline every action verb in the outcomes before you plan a single class — they tell you the cognitive level to aim for.

This is constructive alignment in practice.

For each class activity, name the outcome it serves. If you can't, question whether it earns its place.

Match your expectations of independence to the level indicators.

Ask: am I doing the thinking for them, or designing tasks that make them do it at the right level?

Do the credit-to-hours maths before finalising your assessment brief.

Time yourself doing a scaled-down version of the task, then multiply — a quick reality check on load.

Your industry background gives you a genuine advantage on questions one and two — you already know what capable, independent judgement looks like in your field.

Section 7 of 7

Summary and reflective activity

~1.5 min read

The module descriptor is your map. Learning outcomes are your destination. NFQ level sets your altitude. ECTS credits tell you how far the journey should take — and how it compares to journeys taken elsewhere in Europe.

Let's recap

Module descriptor
An approved document defining what a module covers, how it's taught, and how it's assessed.
Learning outcomes
Describe demonstrable capability, not content covered. Bloom's Taxonomy helps you read the level of thinking required; Biggs' constructive alignment keeps outcomes, teaching, and assessment pointed in the same direction.
NFQ levels
And their alignment with the EQF — set the standard for independence, complexity, and critical judgement expected at each stage, with QQI's Awards Standards translating this into discipline-specific detail.
ECTS credits
Represent total student effort, not contact time, and underpin the comparability of qualifications across Europe, while also offering a practical anchor for realistic assessment design.
